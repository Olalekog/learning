# Interview & Presence Improvement Plan

Personal self-assessment: three related delivery habits to work on
before interviews — staying physically stationary, speaking clearly/
sharply/coherently, and articulation. Companion to
[Elevator-Pitch.md](Elevator-Pitch.md) in this folder.

## 1. The "Greatest Weakness" Answer

All three items are really one weakness — **delivery under pressure**,
not knowledge or substance — which is actually the easiest kind of
weakness to talk about credibly in an interview, because the fix is
observable and already in progress:

> "Something I've been actively working on is delivery — staying
> physically settled instead of pacing or fidgeting when I'm thinking
> on my feet, and making sure my answers come out as clearly and
> concisely as I actually think them, rather than getting long-winded
> under pressure. It's not a knowledge gap, it's a presentation habit —
> so I've been practicing by recording mock answers, reviewing them, and
> deliberately structuring responses (situation, action, result) before
> I start talking instead of thinking out loud. It's made a real,
> measurable difference in how concise and settled my answers are."

~90 words, ~35-40 seconds spoken. Deliver it matter-of-factly, not
apologetically — naming a real, specific weakness with a concrete,
already-in-motion fix is what this question is actually testing for.

## 2. Improvement Plan Per Flaw

### Be stationary

**What's happening**: pacing, shifting weight, fidgeting with hands, or
excessive gesturing while thinking — reads as nervous energy even when
the content of the answer is solid.

**How to fix it**:
- **Plant your stance deliberately** before you start speaking — feet
  shoulder-width, weight even, hands resting on the table or loosely at
  your sides. Decide this *before* the question lands, not while
  answering.
- **Use a pause instead of a movement** as your "thinking" tell — when
  the instinct to shift or pace hits, take a breath and a beat of
  silence instead. A pause reads as composed; movement reads as
  anxious.
- **Record yourself** answering 3-5 practice questions on video, watch
  with the sound off first — movement is far more obvious watching
  yourself than it feels while doing it.
- **For video interviews specifically**: sit slightly further back from
  the camera than feels natural, hands visible on the desk — it
  physically limits the range of fidgeting and looks more settled on
  camera.

### Speak clearly, sharply, and coherently

**What's happening**: likely rambling or losing the thread under
pressure — starting an answer before the structure is clear, so it
wanders before landing the point.

**How to fix it**:
- **Structure before you speak**: silently frame the answer's shape
  (STAR, or Point → Reason → Example → Point) in the first two seconds
  of silence before starting — a half-second pause up front saves a
  much longer, wandering answer.
- **One breath, one point**: if an answer needs more than one breath to
  say, it's probably covering more than one point — a sign to trim, not
  a sign to talk faster.
- **Practice the concise version first**: for any prepared story (see
  [STAR-Scenarios.md](Tech_Consulting/STAR-Scenarios.md) for the
  format), practice the 20-second version before the 60-second one —
  it's much easier to expand a tight answer than to tighten a rambling
  one live.
- **Cut filler starts**: "So, um, I think basically..." — replace with
  starting directly on the content word. Notice it in recordings before
  trying to suppress it live.

### Improve articulation

**What's happening**: likely a pace/enunciation issue — words running
together or trailing off, especially on longer or more technical terms.

**How to fix it**:
- **Slow down 10-15% from what feels natural** — under pressure, most
  people speed up without noticing; deliberately speaking slightly
  slower than feels comfortable usually lands at a normal, clear pace
  to the listener.
- **Over-enunciate in practice, not in the real interview** — practicing
  with exaggerated clarity (even mouth exercises/tongue twisters before
  a call) trains the muscle memory so normal speech comes out clearer
  without consciously "performing" it live.
- **Record and transcribe**: run a practice answer through a
  transcription tool — wherever the transcript looks wrong or garbled
  is exactly where articulation broke down; go back and isolate that
  specific word or phrase.
- **Breathe from a full breath, not a shallow one**, before starting an
  answer — a shallow breath produces a thinner, less controlled voice
  that trails off toward the end of sentences.

## 3. Practice Routine

A tight, repeatable loop beats occasional long practice sessions:

1. Pick one question from
   [Behavioral.md](Tech_Consulting/Behavioral.md) or your own
   [Elevator-Pitch.md](Elevator-Pitch.md).
2. Answer it on video, seated, hands visible, aiming for the concise
   version first.
3. Watch it back **muted first** — score only stillness/posture.
4. Watch it back **with sound** — score only pace/articulation.
5. Re-record the same answer once, applying one fix at a time rather
   than all three at once — it's easier to notice improvement on a
   single dimension per pass.

Five minutes a day of this beats one long session a week — the goal is
building a physical habit, not memorizing a script.
