## 1 Leadership style

**Question:** How would you describe your leadership style?

“My leadership style combines clear direction, collaboration, and accountability. I explain the outcome we need, the constraints we must respect, and where engineers have flexibility.

My platform work at Truist required developing patterns that other teams could adopt, so technical decisions needed to be understandable and practical.

I encourage engineers to challenge assumptions and contribute alternatives. Once we agree on a direction, I make ownership and acceptance criteria clear. I also stay close enough to implementation to recognize when the design needs adjustment.”

## 2 Managing a transformation team

**Question:** How do you manage a team delivering a complex transformation?

“I would translate the transformation into clear workstreams: discovery, architecture, automation, validation, and migration preparation.

Each workstream would have an owner, dependencies, and acceptance criteria. I would use planning sessions to agree on achievable commitments and stand-ups to identify blockers and decisions.

I would make cross-team dependencies visible, particularly identity and networking approvals. My responsibility as a technical lead would be to maintain direction, remove obstacles, and ensure individual tasks contribute to a working platform.”

## 3 Team member motivation

**Question:** How do you motivate team members?

“I start by understanding what motivates each person—technical depth, ownership, learning, or visible business impact.

I connect their work to a meaningful outcome and give them an appropriate level of responsibility. An engineer developing a module should understand which teams will use it and what problem it solves.

I recognize specific contributions and make progress visible. When motivation drops, I investigate the cause, such as unclear priorities, repeated interruptions, or limited growth opportunities, before assuming it is an attitude problem.”

## 4 Team member growth

**Question:** How do you support a team member’s professional growth?

“I would agree on a development goal and create opportunities to practice through real work.

An engineer developing architecture skills could own a design proposal, compare options, and present the recommendation. Someone strengthening Terraform skills could own a module and its validation with support from a more experienced engineer.

I would provide regular feedback and gradually increase responsibility. Growth should be demonstrated through stronger independent judgment and delivery, rather than only course completion.”

## 5 Delegation and accountability

**Question:** How do you delegate without losing accountability?

“I delegate an outcome with context rather than simply assign a task. I explain requirements, decision boundaries, dependencies, and the definition of done.

I agree on checkpoints based on risk and experience. Routine implementation may need light review, while identity or production-network changes require closer collaboration.

The engineer should have room to make decisions, but I remain accountable for coordination and the overall result. I avoid taking work back unnecessarily when coaching would help them complete it.”

## 6 Team conflict resolution

**Question:** Tell me about resolving conflict between team members.

**Practice scenario**

“If two engineers disagreed about a Terraform design, I would first understand the technical concerns and whether communication had become personal.

I would bring the discussion back to shared criteria: maintainability, security, ownership, testing, and delivery time. Where evidence was missing, I would propose a small experiment.

We would record the decision and tradeoffs, then agree how to proceed. My goal would be to resolve the issue while preserving a working relationship and a fair decision-making process.”

## 7 Underperformance

**Question:** How would you handle an underperforming team member?

“I would address the issue privately using specific examples and clear expectations. First, I would understand whether the cause was a skills gap, unclear requirements, workload, or another obstacle.

We would agree on practical improvements, support, and a review date. That might include smaller deliverables, pairing, or clearer acceptance criteria.

As a technical lead, I would coordinate with the person’s manager where formal performance management was required. I would provide support while keeping delivery responsibilities transparent.”

## 8 Difficult feedback

**Question:** How do you give difficult feedback?

“I describe the behavior and impact without making assumptions about intent.

For example, I might explain that an undocumented infrastructure change created deployment uncertainty and made recovery harder. I would ask for the engineer’s perspective, then agree on the expected process.

Feedback should result in a specific next step. I also check whether the team’s workflow contributed, because unclear procedures can create repeated mistakes across otherwise capable engineers.”

## 9 Disagreement with a skilled engineer

**Question:** How do you manage a highly skilled engineer who disagrees with you?

“I would ask them to explain their concerns and alternative. Strong technical disagreement can improve a design.

We would compare options against requirements and examine evidence, including complexity and ownership. If their approach was stronger, I would change my recommendation.

If we still disagreed, I would use the agreed decision authority and document the rationale. I would expect professional commitment to the final decision while keeping a path to revisit it if evidence changes.”

## 10 Security and application team conflict

**Question:** How do you resolve conflict between security and application teams?

“I translate each position into its underlying requirement. Security may need a control, while the application team may be concerned about delays or compatibility.

I would bring both teams together to examine the risk and identify an implementation with manageable friction. Automation can often make the control easier to follow.

Where a gap remains, I would document the exception, compensating controls, owner, and review date. The objective is an explicit risk decision with shared understanding.”

## 11 Risk management

**Question:** How do you manage project risk?

“I maintain a risk register connecting technical uncertainty to delivery or business impact.

For this transformation, examples include undocumented dependencies, limited access, unclear shared-service ownership, and the large application’s migration complexity.

Each material risk needs an owner, mitigation, and escalation trigger. I distinguish risks from issues that have occurred and update stakeholders as evidence changes.

Risk management should affect the plan—for example, moving discovery earlier or choosing a safer pilot.”

## 12 Taking a calculated risk

**Question:** Tell me about a time you would take a calculated risk.

**Practice scenario**

“I would consider a controlled pilot of a new provisioning pattern when it could materially improve repeatability, even if the team had not used the approach before.

I would limit scope, define success and stop conditions, establish recovery, and involve necessary owners. A noncritical environment could provide evidence before production adoption.

I would accept uncertainty where value justified it and consequences were bounded. I would avoid treating a fixed deadline as justification for an uncontrolled production change.”

## 13 When to stop a change

**Question:** When would you refuse to proceed with a change?

“I would stop or escalate when consequences could be serious and essential safeguards were missing—for example, unclear ownership, unknown dependencies, or no credible recovery approach.

I would explain the specific concern and offer a path forward, such as validation, smaller scope, or a different window.

My role is to make risk understandable and help resolve it. If an authorized risk owner accepted residual risk, I would ensure the decision and operating conditions were recorded.”

## 16 Incident communication

**Question:** How do you communicate during a production incident?

“I communicate confirmed impact, actions underway, and the next update time. I distinguish facts from hypotheses and avoid unsupported recovery promises.

Engineers need technical coordination, while business stakeholders need service impact and decisions. I tailor detail accordingly.

I maintain a decision timeline so handovers and the review are accurate. After restoration, I explain verification results and outstanding risks rather than declaring success solely because an alert cleared.”

## 17 Mistakes and lessons learned

**Question:** Tell me about a mistake and what you learned.

**Practice scenario**

“A useful example would be a platform design that was technically complete but insufficiently tested against the application team’s workflow.

I would take responsibility for involving consumers too late, gather feedback, and simplify the interface around common requirements. I would validate the revision with a pilot before expanding adoption.

The lesson is that usability and ownership are architecture concerns. I would demonstrate the change in my approach through earlier stakeholder involvement and clearer acceptance criteria.”

Use a real mistake and actual corrective action before presenting this as a past event.

## 18 Changing your mind

**Question:** Tell me about a time you changed your mind.

**Practice scenario**

“I might initially recommend separating workloads into more subscriptions for clearer boundaries. Discovery could show shared dependencies and constraints that make immediate separation unnecessarily risky.

I would revise the recommendation, explain the evidence, and propose staged separation.

Changing direction should be based on better information, with consequences communicated clearly. I would retain the requirement while adjusting implementation and timeline. The important point is accountability for the outcome rather than defending my first design.”

## 19 Competing stakeholder priorities

**Question:** How do you handle competing stakeholder priorities?

“I compare requests using business impact, security exposure, reliability risk, dependencies, and effort.

I make criteria visible and explain what accepting one request means for others. Where priorities require a business decision, I bring options to the accountable stakeholder.

For this engagement, I would protect agreed foundation outcomes while making migration requests explicit scope decisions. Once priorities are agreed, I communicate the sequence and revisit it when material conditions change.”

## 20 Unrealistic deadlines

**Question:** How do you handle an unrealistic deadline?

“I break the request into outcomes, identify dependencies, and provide evidence-based options.

If full migration by December were unrealistic, I would explain what could be delivered confidently—such as assessed dependencies, validated foundations, and possibly a pilot migration.

I would identify what requires more time, people, or reduced requirements. The discussion should produce an agreed plan and accepted tradeoffs. I would raise uncertainty early rather than wait until the deadline is already at risk.”

## 21 Influence without direct authority

**Question:** How do you influence stakeholders without direct authority?

**Resume-based foundation: Truist**

“At Truist, I developed reusable cloud patterns that teams across the bank followed. That required making architecture useful beyond my immediate implementation.

I connect recommendations to the consuming team’s requirements and explain operational benefits and constraints. I listen for gaps requiring improvement to the standard.

In the interview, I would describe one actual team’s adoption: what it needed, how we aligned the pattern, and the outcome. Influence is demonstrated through adoption and shared decisions.”

## 22 Managing ambiguity

**Question:** How do you manage ambiguity?

“I separate what we know from assumptions and identify decisions that depend on missing information.

I ask focused questions, assign investigation owners, and use prototypes where they resolve uncertainty efficiently. I document assumptions and avoid expensive commitments prematurely.

For the nine-subscription estate, discovery may uncover additional applications or dependencies. I would update architecture and estimates while continuing work that does not depend on those unknowns.”

## 23 Burnout and morale

**Question:** How do you prevent burnout and maintain team morale?

“I look for systemic causes such as excessive work in progress, interruptions, unclear priorities, and concentration of knowledge.

I would make capacity constraints visible, rotate responsibilities where practical, and protect time for documentation and automation that reduce recurring toil.

Recognition matters, but it needs realistic commitments and fair workloads. I encourage early escalation when someone is overloaded so the team can adjust before delivery or wellbeing suffers.”

## 24 Measuring leadership success

**Question:** How do you know you have succeeded as a technical leader?

“I look at delivery outcomes and the team’s ability to sustain them.

Has the platform become more consistent and supportable? Can engineers explain the design, make safe changes, and operate it without relying on one person? Are stakeholders clear about ownership and risks?

For this engagement, success includes validated Terraform foundations, an achievable migration roadmap, and a team able to continue. I assess progress through evidence and feedback rather than the volume of code or documentation.”

## Preparation checklist

- For each past-event question, prepare one real situation, your action, an observed result, and a lesson.
- Use Situation, Task, Action, and Result to keep stories focused.
- Replace practice scenarios with actual experience before presenting them as personal achievements.
- Distinguish technical leadership from formal people-management authority.
- Identify the actual difficult architecture decision and a specific production incident.
- Do not invent incident causes, team counts, savings, recovery times, or direct-report responsibilities.
- Keep the initial answer close to 60 seconds and reserve implementation detail for follow-ups.
- Connect answers to assessment, shared-service ownership, Terraform foundations, governance, and incremental migration.
