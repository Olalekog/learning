## 1 Leadership style

**Question:** How would you describe your leadership style?

“My leadership style is collaborative, but I take ownership of the technical direction and outcome. At Truist Bank, I created reusable AWS and Azure patterns for networking, identity, security, and CI/CD that teams across the bank followed. I also authored reusable Bicep modules for Azure landing zones. That work connects architecture with implementation: teams need standards they can actually use. I explain the reason behind a requirement, listen to implementation concerns, and keep the design connected to security and operational needs. I measure success by consistent delivery and whether other teams can build on the foundation.”

## 3 Team member motivation

**Question:** How do you motivate team members?

“I motivate engineers by making the purpose of their work clear and giving them meaningful ownership. At Rivian, the platforms I worked on supported vehicle telemetry, software services, and factory systems. That gives technical work a concrete purpose: a deployment improvement or monitoring change supports a service people depend on. My approach is to connect an engineer’s task to that outcome, understand what they want to develop, and recognize specific contributions. If progress or motivation drops, I first check for unclear requirements, dependencies, or workload problems rather than assume a lack of commitment.”

## 4 Team member growth

**Question:** How do you support a team member’s professional growth?

“I help engineers grow through progressively greater ownership of real work. My experience at Liberty Mutual included early Azure patterns, monitoring improvements, and recovery documentation, which are good examples of work that requires both technical understanding and clear communication. I would start with a defined component, explain the requirement, review the approach together, and ask the engineer to explain the dependencies and validation. Then I would increase their independence. The goal is someone who can reason through a change and support it, rather than someone who only follows instructions.”

## 5 Delegation and accountability

**Question:** How do you delegate without losing accountability?

“I delegate the outcome, constraints, and acceptance criteria together. At Truist, my work involved reusable infrastructure and cross-team recovery, so unclear boundaries can create problems even when individual technical tasks are correct. For a module change, I would make the consumers, security requirements, and validation expectations clear. I set checkpoints according to the risk and the engineer’s experience, while leaving room for them to solve the problem. I remain accountable for integration and escalation, and I involve the line manager where a decision requires formal management authority.”

## 6 Team conflict resolution

**Question:** Tell me about resolving conflict between team members.

“I bring disagreements back to requirements and evidence. At Regeneron, I combined Terraform and Bicep for infrastructure automation. That experience is relevant because tool boundaries need clear ownership and a maintainable deployment approach. If engineers disagree, I ask each to explain the requirement their design addresses and the consequences of the alternative. We compare resource ownership, operational complexity, testing, and support. Where necessary, I use a small validation exercise. The objective is a decision the team can explain and operate, with the rationale recorded and responsibilities clear.”

## 7 Underperformance

**Question:** How would you handle an underperforming team member?

“I address underperformance privately and focus on specific delivery problems. My Southern Company work included EKS operations and infrastructure and data-pipeline delivery, where delays can come from knowledge gaps, dependencies, or unclear requirements. I would establish which of those is affecting the engineer, agree on a smaller deliverable, and offer pairing or clearer acceptance criteria. I would review progress and reduce support as independence improves. If the issue continued, I would work with their manager using evidence and the support already provided. I would also keep the project’s delivery risk visible.”

## 9 Disagreement with a skilled engineer

**Question:** How do you manage a highly skilled engineer who disagrees with you?

“I am comfortable being challenged by a strong engineer. At Rivian, I worked on EKS and AKS platforms, reusable patterns, controlled upgrades, and reliability goals. Those decisions involve tradeoffs, so a different view can expose something important. I ask the engineer to explain the workload requirement and alternative, then compare the options using reliability, security, complexity, and ownership. If their evidence is stronger, I change my recommendation. If we still disagree, I use the agreed decision authority and document the rationale so the team can move forward professionally.”

## 12 Taking a calculated risk

**Question:** Tell me about a time you would take a calculated risk.

“My Rivian experience included blue-green and canary delivery for vehicle software services, with approvals and environment promotion paths. I accept uncertainty when the potential value is clear and the consequences are controlled.”

## 13 When to stop a change

**Question:** When would you refuse to proceed with a change?

“I pause a change when a material risk has not been addressed or the required authority has not accepted it. At Liberty Mutual, I worked with identity, network isolation, and protection of sensitive insurance data. At Truist, I managed delivery workflows that retained approvals and auditability. Those experiences shape my judgment. I would explain the specific exposure and offer a practical alternative, such as a smaller scope or additional validation. If the issue remained unresolved, I would escalate through the established process and record the decision. A deadline does not remove the need for accountable risk ownership.”

## 16 Incident communication

**Question:** How do you communicate during a production incident?

“I keep incident communication factual and focused on the audience. At TJ Maxx, I participated in incident response during peak retail events, and at Rivian I led incident bridges when services degraded. My approach is to explain confirmed impact, actions underway, and when the next update will come. Technical teams need investigation details, while business stakeholders need service impact and decisions. I distinguish hypotheses from facts and avoid unsupported recovery promises. After restoration, I explain what was checked and any remaining risk, rather than assume that a cleared infrastructure alert proves the application has recovered.”

## 18 Changing your mind

**Question:** Tell me about a time you changed your mind.

“I change my recommendation when new evidence changes the tradeoff. At Regeneron, I designed shared identity and private networking for research workloads across AWS and Azure. My approach is to explain what we learned, retain the original business requirement, and revise the implementation or sequence accordingly. I document the consequences and communicate them to affected owners. Being accountable for architecture means supporting the best decision available, including when it differs from my first recommendation.”

## 23 Burnout and morale

**Question:** How do you prevent burnout and maintain team morale?

“I address morale by looking at workload and recurring operational problems as well as recognition. At Southern Company and Truist, I improved alerts, runbooks, and automation after incidents. As a technical lead, I would make interruptions and capacity constraints visible, protect time for recurring-problem fixes, and encourage knowledge sharing. I would work with the manager when responsibilities need adjustment. Recognition matters, but sustainable commitments and effective support processes are just as important.”
